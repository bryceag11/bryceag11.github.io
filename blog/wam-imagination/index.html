What world action models do with their imagined futures

Where robot policies read their predicted frames, and why that decides where an edit can work

Bryce GrantOctober 20, 2026, updated November 23, 202610 min read

The authors of Sora suggest that at sufficiently large scales video generation models become simulators of the physical world, and give a number of capabilities observed to emerge as model size increases, including 3D consistency, coherence over longer timescales, object permanence, and the ability to perform basic actions that change the state of the world1. All of these seem useful for a robot policy. Hence the assumption underlying world action models (WAMs), robot policies trained to predict future camera frames as well as take an action, is that what is learned from predicting video carries over into a good policy.

All experiments are done on policies that have been publicly released, in simulation with ground-truth knowledge of all object poses. To what extent does the action rely on the predicted frames? Over which denoising steps and network depths? And is the target it acts on depicted in the image or specified in the language instruction? This post follows our paper Where World Action Models Read Their Predicted Frames. At the end we briefly describe the findings of a related paper, Horizon Tomography: Controls That Decide Whether Robot Policies Represent the Future, which looks at the content of these frames.

For all 8 WAMs, knocking out the predicted frames had a greater adverse effect on task success than knocking out the same number of current-frame keys. It also seemed that there were specific sites at which the action reads the predicted frames: the last few denoising steps, for 3 of the 5 models we timed; contiguous layers, for 3 models. Where the target comes from varies between models, and this site decides where edits to the frames will alter the robot's behavior.

Four types of world action models

Zhang et al.2 distinguish 4 main families of WAMs, depending on how the action reaches the predicted future. The read-site paper includes 8 models from the first 3 families. 2 further models fall into the last family, in which predicting the future enters only as a loss term during training, and are considered by horizon tomography. Finally, we include π0.53, which has no world objective at all.

We tested on various settings, including LIBERO14 (LIBERO-10, LIBERO-Object, and the Spatial-OOD layouts15), 24 tasks in RoboCasa kitchens16, a subset of RoboTwin 2.017 with 2 objects, and CALVIN ABC-D chains18. The step, source and mediation tests, along with our predictions and fail conditions, were registered prior to running the counted episodes.

Does the action use its predicted frames?

If the bet pays off anywhere, it would be in how the action reads its predictions of future frames. There are a range of methods which attempt to improve behavior by acting on these predictions: both RoboIRS19 and WA-LQR20 modify internal states during testing; LoopWAM21 and ReGuide22 do additional training using rollouts from the policy itself. All of these will affect the predicted action only via the attention from the action queries to the keys and values (K/V) of the predicted frames, that is, only at the denoising steps and layers where that attention carries information.

We refer to this denoising step and range of layers as the read site. Attention from the action to all future tokens was knocked out by RIFT23 for 4 WAMs, with a corresponding decrease in success. There was no equivalent knockout of current-frame keys to control for the total number of keys, so this may be a contributing factor. For EWAM24, the cost was found to be in the mid-layers. Neither states at which denoising step the frames are read, where the target object in the frames comes from, or how large an effect the frames carry.

The attention scores provide a natural starting point. When performing manipulation tasks, high attention will tend to fall over the gripper. On LIBERO the next object to be moved is almost always the closest object to the gripper, so simply sorting all objects by 3D distance to the gripper gave very high AUCs (.979 to 1.000) across all 10 policies tested. None of the individual heads' gaze scores25 outperformed this simple measure when tested (8 policies). Some form of intervention was thus required to locate the read site.

The read-site protocol uses 4 interventions. For attention knockouts26, all keys corresponding to the set of predicted frames are prevented from being attended to by actions; an equivalent number of keys for the current frame or frames are knocked out in a control. This can be restricted to occur at only 1 denoising step, or over a quarter of the total number of layers, to localize the effect. With an interchange27, actions attend to K/V for the predicted frames from a run under another instruction. Interchanging after re-computing these frames' K/V with the original instruction will therefore show if the target is carried in the frames themselves. Mediation analysis28 returns the fraction of the total effect on the output which is mediated via the predicted frames.

The action needs its frames

All 8 models perform worse when the predicted frames are removed than when an equivalent number of current-frame keys are removed (Figure 3). In particular, 2 of them are completely unsuccessful on LIBERO-10: LingBot-VA drops from 49 to 0 out of 50 successes, compared to 49 for its control, and DreamZero to 0. The same holds on RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa and CALVIN, where VPP sees a large drop, with most chains failing.

Attention masses give no indication of such costs. In the last step, the action queries put about 13% of their attention on Cosmos Policy's predicted frames, compared to 18% for the actual current images. And LingBot-VA is able to complete 44 out of 50 episodes relying solely on its own predicted future when all real frames in its cache have been knocked out.

As all frames were available at train time, fully knocking them out provides an upper bound on the extent to which the action relies on them. Replacing Cosmos Policy's predicted-frame K/V with in-distribution values, namely the mean over tokens, or the prediction made 1 chunk earlier, yielded 41 and 39 successes out of 50 on LIBERO-Object. Both are much higher than 23 for the full knockout, indicating reliance on the current predicted frames, but showing that the knockout overestimates this by a factor of 2.4 to 3.

When and where the action reads them

This occurs towards the end of the denoising process: 3 out of 5 models we timed read their predicted frames in one or more of the very last steps. Removing predicted frames from only the final step out of 5 reduced Cosmos Policy substantially, to 11 out of 50 successful completions on LIBERO-10 (48 with none removed), with all other individual steps giving similar high performance (Figure 4). All episodes fail for DreamZero when the final 2 out of 8 steps are removed, and LaWAM's final 2 out of 10 reproduce its full loss. For Cosmos Policy, the output of this last step is the one the model returns, and the effect followed the returned step at all other noise levels we tried.

This step also specifies which object should be grasped. Interchanging Cosmos Policy's predicted frames only at the last step causes the first grasp to go to the other object in 44 of 50 episodes of LIBERO-Object. If done at steps 1 to 4, no episodes were affected.

It is also narrow in depth, with 3 models reading in one block of consecutive layers. Knocking out only layers 21 to 27 brings Cosmos Policy down to 24 of 50, and knocking out only layers 8 to 14 leaves LingBot-VA with 4 of 50; all other quarters have much smaller effects (Figure 4). DreamZero reads in the latter half of its layers. The Cosmos Policy and LingBot-VA blocks also hold on a second benchmark. However, for LaWAM, InternVLA-A1 and VPP, none of the quarters of layers gives a close match to the full loss.

Where the target comes from

For LIBERO-10, swapping in the predicted-frame K/V causes 39 of 40 episodes for LingBot-VA, and all 40 episodes for DreamZero, to follow the counterfactual. This is comparable to the fraction of redirections if we explicitly supply the counterfactual instruction. In contrast, swapping in the current-frame K/V causes none of the 40 episodes to be redirected, for either model. Therefore, the predicted frames do carry a target.

The target can be either inherent in the image, or induced by the generating instruction. We therefore re-encode the source's predicted frames with the original instruction, that is, without any counterfactual language but with the same image. For Cosmos Policy, the target is carried by the instruction: the source frames redirect 86 of 100 LIBERO-Object episodes, compared to 0 of 100 after re-encoding.

LingBot-VA is at the other extreme, with all but one (39 of 40) of its original redirects preserved following re-encoding. DreamZero is similar, with 59 of 60 preserved. For VPP around .59 of redirects are preserved, compared to the .80 we had registered. Both Cosmos Policy and LingBot-VA jointly denoise an action sequence and video, but differ in where the corresponding target is taken from. Thus, modifying the video will depend on which source each model uses.

How much passes through the frames

We can put one number on this with mediation analysis. For each chunk, we perform 2 runs with identical seeds: save the predicted-frame K/V under 1 set of conditions, and use these K/V in a run under a different set of conditions. The proportion mediated (PM) is the fraction of the conditions' effect on the outcome that is carried by these K/V alone.

As can be seen from Figure 6, the 4 models we measured cover the whole range for an instruction change. Simply interchanging the K/V of the predicted frames in VPP (CALVIN) moves the other block first nearly as often as changing the instruction, a PM of .99. For the foresight tokens of Seer-Large, this is much less, .36; on RoboTwin 2.0 it is .09 for LaWAM's predicted tokens, and .00 for the predicted tokens of InternVLA-A1, although .67 for its current image. Thus about two thirds of the effect of the instruction on the action is mediated via the current image, for InternVLA-A1.

Mediation can also measure a weight change. We trained a LoRA adapter on successful rollouts of the released Cosmos Policy weights in the Spatial-OOD layouts. On 480 test pairs, the released weights get 326 and the adapter gets 414. Giving the released model the adapter's predicted-frame K/V only at the read site (that is, layers 21 to 27 at the last step) gives a PM of .83. The same patch in layers 7 to 13, the registered off-site control, gives a PM of only .02. Hence most of the adapter's gain passes through the predicted frames at the read site.

Further fine-tuning with successful rollouts from the policy itself leads to improved results in the Spatial-OOD layouts. Similarly, probe guidance helps when applied at layer 21. Limiting the adapter to layers 21 to 27 helps no more than limiting it to an earlier range. Of course, these results are for only 1 model and 4 tasks, and fine-tuning in this manner decreases success on RoboCasa and in DreamZero.

What the frames carry

If the action reads the frames, what is in them? The WAM bet is that they hold physics, and the DreamZero authors argue that WAMs learn physical dynamics by predicting future world states5. Our companion paper tests this in the features of 6 LIBERO-10 policies. Against only a linear model of the present, every policy looks like it predicts the future. Once a kernel model of the present scene and the robot's commanded motion are controlled for, what is left about an object's future motion is equivalent to zero at bounds of 0.02 to 0.03 of its variance, for linear codes in the token mean. The future information we can read follows the robot's own plan.

A plan the action consults

Taken together, the predicted frames in these policies work as a plan the action consults at a specific site, with a denoising step, range of layers, and target source that differ between checkpoints.

First, editing predictions will only have an effect if the edit reaches the point at which values are read. So for Cosmos Policy this is the final denoising iteration, editing the corresponding instruction as well; and for LingBot-VA any such edit to the frames would be permanent. With InternVLA-A1 it would be appropriate to act on the current image to follow the desired instruction.

All numbers reported here come from simulation with published checkpoints. There were 5 models tested with the step test, 6 with the layer test (a single range for 3 of these), and 4 with the source test. Each policy tested is associated with one checkpoint, with corresponding changes to backbone and training distribution, so any comparison between different policies should only be taken descriptively. The next step is to perform the knockout and interchange tests on a physical arm.
