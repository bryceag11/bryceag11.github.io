What world action models do with the futures they imagine

Bryce GrantOctober 20, 20269 min read

A world action model (WAM) is a robot policy that learns to predict the future, usually as video, along with its actions. The usual explanation for their gains is that predicting the future teaches the policy how the world works. We tested WAMs in simulation, where every object's pose is known, asking whether the action uses the future the model imagines and whether the policy's features hold the future beyond what the present state already determines. World action models act on the plan they imagine.

In seven of the eight WAMs whose action can read a predicted future, masking that future cuts success far below a current-frame mask, on LIBERO-10, RoboTwin 2.0 or CALVIN. Swapping that future changes which object the robot grabs first, and steering the plan toward the instructed object raises Cosmos Policy's success out of distribution. On LIBERO-10, once we control for the present state and the robot's own motion, the features of six policies hold at most 0.003 more R2 for a target's future displacement.

WAM taxonomy

Zhang et al.1 sort WAMs into four paradigms, and we add latent foresight for two of our models.

The wiring decides which tests apply. We intervened in three of the four families, on LIBERO11, RoboTwin 2.012 and CALVIN13, and scored gaze heads on BEHAVIOR-1K and DROID as well.

The gripper confound

In manipulation the object that moves next is the one in or beside the gripper, so the gaze-head score of Gandikota and Bau14 selects heads that follow the hand: distance to the gripper alone finds the next object at AUC .979 to 1.000 in all ten LIBERO policies we scored15,16,17, and the score correlates with a held-object score at Spearman .90 to 1.00. We call the heads it picks carry heads. RIFT18 masks the whole predicted-frame cache of four WAMs and finds that success drops. A whole-cache mask names no layer or head and says nothing about what the read carries.

Read interventions

We call the action tokens' attention to the predicted frames the action's read of the future. A controlled gaze score counts a head as anticipatory only on frames where the next object is far from the gripper, against a distance-matched null. Size-matched blinding masks the read in every layer and head and, as the control, the same number of current-frame tokens. Restricted masks take one band of layers or k heads at a time. A key/value swap runs a second pass under a counterfactual instruction and copies only its predicted frames' keys and values into the action pass. Figure 1 shows the read in three models, with five Cosmos Policy tracker heads overlaid, the carry heads whose attention inside the predicted frames best follows the gripper.

Anticipatory heads

With the gripper confound controlled, every simulated policy we scored has heads that prefer the next object before the hand reaches it: 2 to 248 heads per LIBERO policy, 38 to 162 in each of six RoboTwin policies (distance-matched top AUC .91 to .97), all 48 perceiver heads of VPP on CALVIN (.73 to .82), and 120 of 144 π0.519 heads on BEHAVIOR-1K20 (top .890). On recorded DROID21 frames, 3 of 144 π0.5-DROID heads pass the distance-matched null (top .639). Outside π0.5 these heads are entangled with instruction mentions, so the interventions below use carry and tracker heads.

Masking the predicted frames

On LIBERO-10 (50 episodes per arm), masking the read drops success from 48 to 10 in Cosmos Policy, 49 to 0 in LingBot-VA and 35 to 0 in DreamZero, and the size-matched current-frame masks leave 34, 49 and 24. On RoboTwin 2.0 (60 per arm), LingBot-VA drops from 52 to 0 against 50 for the current mask, InternVLA-A1 from 44 to 11 against 39, LaWAM from 55 to 36 against 57 and Motus from 45 to 0 against 27 (Table 1). On CALVIN, VPP falls from 4.16 to 0.01 tasks per chain, against 4.12 for the smaller current-frame mask (Figure 2).

Masking a quarter of the layers at a time locates a read band in each model, and inside it the top 100 tracker heads carry most of the effect where 100 random heads do little (Figure 2, One band and Tracker heads). On RoboTwin 2.0, masking LingBot-VA's layers 7 to 14 alone gives 0 of 20, as masking every layer does, and the other bands leave 18 to 20. In DreamZero three heads are sufficient to block the read: on held-out initial states, masking their future reads leaves 1 of 50 successes, against 31 of 50 for their current-frame reads (Fisher p = 2×10−11).

The band and its heads form when the model learns to act. Under the Cosmos-Predict2 weights, Cosmos Policy's top 50 tracker heads score at the level of random heads, and action fine-tuning raises the action's attention share on the predicted frames in layers 21 to 27 from .058 to .132. LingBot-VA forms its band earlier, in robot video-action pretraining.

Swapping the imagined future

On two LIBERO-10 basket tasks, LingBot-VA moves the counterfactual object first in 39 of 40 episodes and DreamZero in 40 of 40. The same swap on current-frame keys and values moves it in 0 of 40 (McNemar p = 4×10−12 and 2×10−12), and the swapped runs fail the original task in all 40. Re-encoding LingBot-VA's counterfactual frames under the original instruction still redirects 39 of 40, so the imagined video carries the plan. On LIBERO-Object the swap redirects 179 of 200 Cosmos Policy episodes and 199 of 200 DreamZero. On RoboTwin 2.0, LingBot-VA grabs it first in 39 of 60 swapped episodes, against 5 of 60 with the current-frame swap and 40 of 60 under the counterfactual instruction (McNemar p = 5×10−9). Cosmos Policy is the weakest redirector, 10 of 40 on LIBERO-10.

On LIBERO-10, the read band alone redirects 21 of 40 DreamZero episodes against 40 for all layers. On 40 fresh LingBot-VA episodes, where the all-layer swap redirects all 40, the band redirects 19 and the top 100 tracker heads 30. On RoboTwin 2.0 the band alone carries the redirect: in 80 fresh episodes, LingBot-VA's layers 8 to 14 redirect 57, against 59 for all layers and 5 for layers 15 to 21 (Restricted swaps). ForeWAM22 builds a model around this key-value channel.

Guidance out of distribution

Out of distribution, Cosmos Policy often imagines the wrong target. Told to put the bowl on the cookie box, it imagines the bowl on the plate and the action follows. Temporal Ratio23 scales language and plan guidance by how strongly the action attends to future frames (TR-AG). With settings frozen in-distribution, it lowers success on the compositional LIBERO Spatial-OOD suite24 from 171 to 139 of 600 paired episodes (Holm p = .002), and our tracker-head variants stay at or below base (157 and 169).

A better plan through the same channel helps. Frames imagined during a successful rollout of the same or nearest scene, re-noised to the level Cosmos's action reads (σ ≥ 4), raise success from 171 to 182 of 240 (12 gained, 1 lost, Holm p = .007). Those plans were chosen with outcome knowledge, and an outcome-trained critic lifts best-of-8 success only from 168 to 172 of 240, since the sampler-seed candidates barely differ.

Guiding the imagined target works. A probe at layer 21, inside the read band, reads which object the imagined frames head for. At the last two of Cosmos's five denoising calls, and only in chunks where a second probe flags the plan as headed elsewhere, we move the imagined frames along the gradient of that probe toward the instructed object. On a held-out split registered in advance, success rises from 104 to 122 of 160 scenes, 11 points (24 gained, 6 lost), and on 240 fresh scenes it rises from 166 to 176 (15 gained, 5 lost). With a second sampler seed on the held-out split it rises from 103 to 134. Where the base run hovers above the bowl, the guided run lifts it and places it on the plate. The same push at the first three calls leaves success at base, and the timing has a reason. Masking the imagined frames at only one denoising call shows when the action reads them: in Cosmos only the last call matters (11 of 50 LIBERO-10 episodes succeed against 48), and DreamZero likewise reads at its last two denoiser steps. Swapping DreamZero's imagined plan for another instruction's at only those last two steps sends the arm to the other object in 18 of 20 episodes, and the same swap at any earlier step never does. Cosmos behaves the same way: a swap at its last call alone redirects 18 of 20 grasps, and a swap at the first four redirects none. LingBot-VA finishes its video before its 50-step action loop begins, and there the two roles come apart. Blinding the first 10 action steps breaks the task, yet a swapped plan seen only in those steps redirects no grasp, while one seen in steps 10 to 49 redirects all 20. The early steps need a plan to start a motion, and the later steps pick the object. In all three models, the target is read late in action denoising, which is where a correction works. Adding the object direction to the residual at layer 23 in the same chunks gains 8 points (117 of 160). Ungated steering helps at no strength we tuned, and steering the tracker heads leaves success at base.

Horizon tomography

The Fast-WAM9 authors suggest the main value of video prediction “may lie in improving world representations during training”. Horizon tomography tests that credit: a probe predicts a target object's displacement δ steps ahead from the activations, and we report its cross-fitted gain over a nested control ck, a conditional probe25:

$$\Delta R^2_k(\delta) \;=\; R^2\!\left(y_\delta \mid c_k,\, h\right) \;-\; R^2\!\left(y_\delta \mid c_k\right)$$

Here c1 models the present scene, c2 the full simulator state, and c3 adds the robot's own upcoming motion. The test has a blind spot by construction: under a fixed policy in a deterministic simulator, anything an internal dynamics model predicts is a function of the state, so c2 absorbs it.

At δ = 16 on LIBERO-10, six policies score 0.46 to 0.51 beyond the present scene, 0.001 to 0.021 beyond the full state, and within 0.003 of zero once their own motion enters the control. π0.5, trained with no world objective, carries as much beyond the state as the pixel-objective Fast-WAM, 0.021 [0.015, 0.028] against 0.019 [0.013, 0.026]. RoboTwin 2.0 repeats the pattern for Fast-WAM and π0.5 at δ = 16 and for LingBot-VA at δ = 32. Beyond the commanded motion the residual is at most 0.009 on LIBERO-Spatial and LIBERO-Goal (500 episodes). In matched LeWorldModel26 retrains on Push-T, a world objective added to behavior cloning raises the residual beyond state and motion by 0.0057 at the seed level, and no retrain reads hidden mass or friction.

Summary and limits

For the models we tested, the imagined future's keys and values are a test-time handle on object choice, and the plan's target is the lever: the action follows whatever plan reaches it, and steering that target raised success where a stronger read lowered it. On LIBERO-10 the ladder finds no extra world knowledge behind the gains from video training, which fits Yu's27 proof that world-action imitation and behavior cloning share their population action target, and ARPS's28 finding that future supervision helps the action under shift. Richens et al.29 prove that agents generalizing across many goals contain a world model, a premise ten fine-tuning tasks do not meet. Probes and steering have reached VLAs30,31,32,33 (our six-VLA study: paper, Action Atlas), and Molinari et al.34 find a world model in OpenVLA against a raw-embedding baseline without our controls.

Every intervention ran in simulation. The blocking heads and most restricted swaps come from LIBERO-10, where 96 to 99 percent of target motion happens under contact, and the ladder's RoboTwin replication covers three policies. Next: interventions on a physical arm.
