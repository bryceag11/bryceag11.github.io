What world action models do with their imagined futures

Bryce GrantOctober 20, 20269 min read

World action models (WAM) are robot policies that try to learn the future typically in the form of video and their actions. A common intuition is that learning to predict the future will allow the policy to understand how the world behaves. We test this hypothesis on public checkpoints within simulation environments that have access to ground truth pose of all objects. Can we tell if the action utilizes the future the model has imagined? Do the features of the policy represent the future more than the present allows? World action models follow the plan they have imagined.

For 7 out of the 8 world action models that can access a predicted future we find that masking that future results in lower success rates compared to masking the current frame on LIBERO-10, RoboTwin 2.0 or CALVIN. By changing the future we can cause the robot to pick up different objects and by guiding the predicted plan towards the correct object we can improve Cosmos Policies out of distribution performance. On LIBERO-10 the amount of additional R2 the target objects future displacement is represented in is at most .003 after accounting for current state and the actions taken by the robot.

Four Types of World Action Models

We can classify world action models into four categories based on the work by Zhang et al.1. We will include latent foresight for 2 of our world action models.

Based on the architecture we are able to test some of these hypotheses. We were able to intervene on three out of the four types of world action models on LIBERO11, RoboTwin 2.012 and CALVIN13. We also evaluate the gaze heads on BEHAVIOR-1K and DROID.

The gripper confound

Since whatever is next to be manipulated will be near or in the gripper the gaze-head score from Gandikota and Bau14 which targets heads looking after the hand: just distance to the gripper identifies the next object with an AUC between .979 and 1.000 across all ten LIBERO policies that we score15,16,17 and is correlated with a held object score from Spearman .90 to 1.00. We refer to these heads as carry heads. When we mask out all the WAM's four future frames in RIFT18 we see performance drop. Masking all future frames doesn't point to a specific layer or head or what information they are reading from the future.

Scoring the read

We define the attention of the actions onto the predicted frames as the actions read on the future. Using our controlled gaze score we can identify heads that look at the future only when the next object is away from the gripper compared to a distance matched baseline. Our size matched blinding masks the read in all layers and heads as well as masking an equal number of current frames as a control. We can also mask limited ranges of layers or k amount of heads. Our key value swap takes another forward pass of the model with a different instruction and copies only the key and values of the predicted frames over to our actions pass. Figure 1 visualizes the read for three models. We have highlighted the Cosmos Policy tracker heads which are the carry heads that look at the gripper the most within the predicted frames.

Heads that anticipate

Once we account for the gripper confound all simulation policies we have scored contain heads that look at the next object before the gripper: Between 2 and 248 heads per LIBERO policy 38-162 per RoboTwin policy across the six we score (top AUC distance matched .91-.97), all 48 heads in VPP's perceiver for CALVIN (.73-.82) and 120/144 heads in π0.519 on BEHAVIOR-1K20 (top .890). For real world data in DROID21 we find 3/144 heads in π0.5-DROID that beat our distance matched baseline (top .639). As these heads are correlated to instructions outside of π0.5 we use the carry and tracker heads for our interventions.

Masking future frames

Masking the read decreases success rate on LIBERO-10 (50 episodes for each arm) from 48 to 10 for Cosmos Policy, 49 to 0 for LingBot-VA and 35 to 0 for DreamZero while the size matched current frame masks result in 34, 49 and 24. For RoboTwin 2.0 (60 episodes for each arm) LingBot-VA drops from 52 to 0 compared to 50 for the current frame mask. InternVLA-A1 drops from 44 to 11 compared to 39, LaWAM from 55 to 36 compared to 57 and Motus from 45 to 0 compared to 27 (Table 1). VPP drops from 4.16 to 0.01 average tasks completed per sequence on CALVIN compared to 4.12 when we mask the same number of current frames (Figure 2).

By removing the keys we are also causing the model to go out of distribution from its input so these numbers may overestimate how reliant the actions are on the plan. On LIBERO-Object when we mask Cosmos Policy's future frames we get 23 out of 50 paired episodes compared to 50 when unmasked. If we replace the keys and values with the average of their tokens we get 41 and if we replace them with the previous chunks plan we get 39. So there is roughly a 10 out of 50 difference from the content of the plan the model is currently using.

When we mask 1/4 of the layers at a time we find a band of layers that contain the read for each model. Within this band we find that the top 100 tracker heads result in a similar drop as masking all heads while 100 random heads do not (Figure 2 One band and Tracker heads). For RoboTwin 2.0 when we only mask layers 7-14 of LingBot-VA we get 0/20 success just as masking all layers does. All other bands result in 18-20 successes. For DreamZero masking three heads is enough to stop the read: when we test on unseen starting states we find 1/50 successes when we mask the future frames for these three heads compared to 31/50 when masking the current frames (Fisher p=2×10−11).

The read develops as the model is trained to take actions. Before training the Cosmos Policy model on actions it has the same score as random heads for its top 50 tracker heads. After fine tuning the action's attention to the predicted frames increases from .058 to .132 in layers 21-27. LingBot-VA develops this band during it's robot video action pretraining.

Swapping the imagined future

For 2 LIBERO-10 tasks that involve baskets LingBot-VA reaches for the counterfactual object first in 39/40 trials. DreamZero reaches for the counterfactual object first in 40/40 trials. When using the current frame keys and values to swap we get 0/40 (McNemar p = 4×10−12 and 2×10−12). All 40 of these failed to complete the original task. We also tried this on Cosmos Policy with LIBERO-Object. If we take another pass with the same instructions but different sampler seeds there was no effect on 50/50 trials. However if we use the keys and values from a pass with a counterfactual prompt we get 45/50 trials reaching for the counterfactual object first. This shows that the swap is happening due to the information within the plan. If we take LingBot-VAs counterfactual frames and encode them with the correct instruction it will still reach for the counterfactual object first 39/40. Showing that the video is what contains the information about the plan. For LIBERO-Object we can get 179/200 Cosmos Policy episodes and 199/200 DreamZero to reach for the counterfactual object first. When doing this on RoboTwin 2.0 LingBot-VA reaches for the counterfactual object first in 39/60 trials. Using the current frame gets us 5/60 and the counterfactual prompt 40/60 (McNemar p = 5×10−9). Cosmos Policy had the least amount of trials it could get to reach for the counterfactual object first at 10/40.

When only using the layers that contain our read band we can get DreamZero to reach for the counterfactual object first in 21/40 trials on LIBERO-10. Compared to all layers which is 40/40. On 40 new LingBot-VA trials that have the ability to reach for the counterfactual object first when swapping all layers we find that using only the band we can get 19/40. Using the top 100 tracker heads we can get 30/40. We were unable to find a band of layers or set of heads that could reach our predetermined threshold of 35/40. On RoboTwin 2.0 we are able to use just the layers in the band to get our desired result. Over 80 new trials LingBot-VA layers 8-14 are able to reach for the counterfactual object first 57/80. While all layers can reach 59/80 and layers 15-21 only reach 5/80 (Restricted swaps). ForeWAM22 uses this layer to help create their model.

Out of distribution guidance

When operating out of distribution Cosmos will frequently hallucinate the incorrect object. If prompted to place the bowl on the cookie box it will imagine the bowl on top of the plate and perform the action. Scaling guidance by attention to future frames (Temporal Ratio23) (TR-AG) can scale both language and plan guidance. When using these values obtained from in-distribution performance we see TR-AG decrease performance on the compositional LIBERO Spatial-OOD benchmark suite24. From 171 successes to 139 out of 600 pair episodes (holm p=.002). Our tracker head implementations perform no better than base (157 and 169 successes).

Guiding with a better plan works. We take frames from successful rollouts of the same or closest scene. Re noise these to the level that Cosomos actions are reading from (σ≥4) we see an increase from 171 to 182 successes out of 240. (Gained 12 lost 1 holm p=.007). Since these plans were selected based on their outcome we also train a critic on outcomes to improve selection. Using this critic to select the best plan out of 8 increases our success rate from 168 to 172 out of 240. Our sampler seed has little effect on what plans are sampled.

Steering the imagined frames near the end of generation. We can probe the network at layer 21 (inside of where actions are being read) to identify what object the imagined frames are traveling towards. During the final 2 out of 5 sampling steps we add the gradient of this probe towards the desired object if our second probe identifies the plan is heading towards something else. On a holdout set that we registered before running we see our success rate increase from 104 to 122 out of 160 scenes. An improvement of 11 points (24 gained 6 lost). On our registered new test set we see our success rate go from 108 to 116 out of 160 (11 gained 3 lost p=.057). Adding a second sampler seed on our holdout set sees our success rate go from 103 to 134. Here we can see our model lifting the bowl and placing it on top of the plate compared to our base model who just hovers over the bowl. The direction we add this to doesn't matter much. Adding a random direction with our gating and magnitude will yield 118/160 successes. If we guide opposite to our probed direction we see 17 successes gained and 13 lost (p=.58). Our probed direction causes less failures of previously successful examples (6 vs 14) but seems to be the perturbation late in the plan that is allowing us to gain these successes. If we add this signal during the first 3 calls we see no change in success rate. There is a reason for this. If we mask the imagined frames during 1 sampling step we can see what step actions are being informed by them. For Cosmos this is only the last sampling step. 11 out of 50 LIBERO-10 examples succeed when only the final sampling step has access to the plan. Dreamzero also shows this behavior by informing actions during it's last 2 denoiser steps. If we replace Dreamzeros plan with a different instructions during these final 2 steps we can see our arm travel to that objects 18/20 times. Replacing it during any other step yields 0 successes. We also see this behavior in Cosmos if we replace the plan on it's last sampling step we can direct our arm to the other object 18/20. Replacing during the first 4 sampling steps yields no successes. This also occurs if we replace the prompt with another instruction on these steps. 20/20 successes when replacing on the last step and 0 when replacing during the first 4 steps. So in Cosmos the plan or prompt on the last step determines what our action is aimed at. This is because the plan is informed by the sampler. With LingBot-VA we can separate this behavior as the video is fully generated before actions begin sampling. If we mask our first 10 actions the model is unable to complete the task. However if we only give it a replaced plan during these 10 actions we see no successes and if we give it a replaced plan on actions 10-49 we see 20/20 successes. It appears that LingBot-VA needs a plan to generate the first few actions then knows what object to target on later steps. As you can see across all of our models they inform what object they should be targeting late into their action sampling. Which is where we can help steer them. Adding our objects to the residual at layer 23 on these steps allows us to gain 8 points on our holdout set. (117/160). This did not generalize to new scenes or our second sampler seed. Ungated steering did not help at any strength we tried. Steering the tracker heads yielded no increase in success rate.

Horizon tomography

The Fast-WAM9 authors suggest the main value of video prediction “may lie in improving world representations during training”. Horizon tomography tests that credit: a probe predicts a target object's displacement δ steps ahead from the activations, and we report its cross-fitted gain over a nested control ck, a conditional probe25:

$$\Delta R^2_k(\delta) \;=\; R^2\!\left(y_\delta \mid c_k,\, h\right) \;-\; R^2\!\left(y_\delta \mid c_k\right)$$

Here c1 models the present scene, c2 the full simulator state, and c3 adds the robot's own upcoming motion. The test has a blind spot by construction: under a fixed policy in a deterministic simulator, anything an internal dynamics model predicts is a function of the state, so c2 absorbs it.

At δ = 16 on LIBERO-10, six policies score 0.46 to 0.51 beyond the present scene, 0.001 to 0.021 beyond the full state, and within 0.003 of zero once their own motion enters the control. π0.5, trained with no world objective, carries as much beyond the state as the pixel-objective Fast-WAM, 0.021 [0.015, 0.028] against 0.019 [0.013, 0.026]. RoboTwin 2.0 repeats the pattern for Fast-WAM and π0.5 at δ = 16 and for LingBot-VA at δ = 32. Beyond the commanded motion the residual is at most 0.009 on LIBERO-Spatial and LIBERO-Goal (500 episodes). In matched LeWorldModel26 retrains on Push-T, a world objective added to behavior cloning raises the residual beyond state and motion by 0.0057 at the seed level, and no retrain reads hidden mass or friction.

Summary and limits

For the models we tested, the imagined future's keys and values are a test-time handle on object choice, and the plan's target is the lever: the action follows whatever plan reaches it, and steering that target raised success where a stronger read lowered it. On LIBERO-10 the ladder finds no extra world knowledge behind the gains from video training, which fits Yu's27 proof that world-action imitation and behavior cloning share their population action target, and ARPS's28 finding that future supervision helps the action under shift. Richens et al.29 prove that agents generalizing across many goals contain a world model, a premise ten fine-tuning tasks do not meet. Probes and steering have reached VLAs30,31,32,33 (our six-VLA study: paper, Action Atlas), and Molinari et al.34 find a world model in OpenVLA against a raw-embedding baseline without our controls.

Every intervention ran in simulation. The blocking heads and most restricted swaps come from LIBERO-10, where 96 to 99 percent of target motion happens under contact, and the ladder's RoboTwin replication covers three policies. Next: interventions on a physical arm.
